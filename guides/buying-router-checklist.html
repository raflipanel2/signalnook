Planning / GUIDE NO. 17

New router buying checklist: beyond the biggest Wi-Fi number

Routers are long-lived infrastructure. Security updates, connection ports and support matter as much as the advertised wireless label.

SignalNook EditorialReviewed 11 Nov 2026
THE SHORT ANSWER

Routers are long-lived infrastructure. Security updates, connection ports and support matter as much as the advertised wireless label. Begin with a repeatable comparison and record one change at a time, rather than making several router changes together.

Write down your actual constraints

List your internet plan, number of active devices, household shape, thick-wall locations, and whether Ethernet or a fiber gateway is already provided. Determine if you need a standalone router, an access point, or a mesh system. Buying a full router when your ISP device cannot be bridged easily can create avoidable double-NAT and administration problems.

Compare support and security

Look for a transparent firmware update policy, a current product support page and WPA3 or supported WPA2/WPA3 modes. Check whether remote administration can be disabled, guest isolation is documented and configuration backup is available. A slightly less flashy router with supported updates may be a better long-term home device than an abandoned top-tier model.

Read the wired specifications

Verify WAN and LAN port speeds against your internet service and local file-transfer needs. A “multi-gig Wi-Fi” headline does not mean the wired internet port can carry that speed. If you have a NAS or wired desktop, check the switch topology and backhaul ports. Remember adapters and cables can also limit measured throughput.

Consider bands and compatibility

Wi-Fi 6, 6E and 7 features require compatible client hardware and locally permitted spectrum. Your older smart devices may still depend on 2.4 GHz, so compatibility and supported security modes remain important. Do not pay a premium solely because a router supports a new band that none of your devices can use.

Plan migration without panic

Before swapping hardware, export settings where supported and document SSID, guest setup and any necessary ISP authentication. Choose a period when internet downtime is acceptable. Test critical devices, work calls, printer discovery and smart-home hubs afterward. Keep the old equipment available briefly if your migration plan permits it.

A worked example

Example: one household has a 200 Mbps plan and an older printer that uses 2.4 GHz. Their priority may be reliable firmware updates and compatible security, not a premium Wi-Fi 7 system with expensive multi-gig ports.

How to interpret three common observations

The same visible symptom can have more than one cause. Use the comparisons below as test priorities, not as an automatic diagnosis of your particular equipment.

Home has 200 Mbps ISP and several old IoT clients

Security updates, 2.4 GHz compatibility and support policy may matter more than advertised multi-gig radio rates.

Home has wired NAS and new multi-gig service

Check WAN, LAN and switch-port throughput, and whether the client hardware supports the target speeds.

ISP gateway already supplies routing

Determine whether an access point is more appropriate than stacking a second unmanaged router.

Field checklist

  • Write down layout and service speed
  • Check updates and warranty/support policy
  • Check WAN and LAN port capacities
  • Confirm smart-device and WPA compatibility
  • Plan a safe migration and rollback
Don't make this mistake

Do not infer real-world internet speed from a router’s large combined-band speed figure.
