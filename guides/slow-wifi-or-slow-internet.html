Slow Wi-Fi or slow internet? Run a useful comparison

A speed-test number by itself does not identify the bottleneck. Separate local wireless performance from your provider connection with repeatable measurements.

SignalNook EditorialReviewed 11 Nov 2026
THE SHORT ANSWER

A speed-test number by itself does not identify the bottleneck. Separate local wireless performance from your provider connection with repeatable measurements. Begin with a repeatable comparison and record one change at a time, rather than making several router changes together.

Choose a baseline that answers one question

Test from the same device at roughly the same time, first near the router and then at the problem location. If you can, add an Ethernet test to the same gateway using a device and port that support your service tier. A wired speed close to your plan with much slower remote-room Wi-Fi points to a local network limitation. Poor wired and wireless results point toward the provider link, a busy gateway, or an upstream equipment problem. One result cannot reliably establish average performance.

Make the tests comparable

Pause large downloads and cloud backups for the first controlled trial. Use the same speed-test service and the same device if possible, note the measurement location, connection type and time, and repeat two or three times. Remember that a mobile device, old Ethernet adapter, or 100 Mbps switch can limit the measurement independently of the ISP. Avoid comparing an expensive laptop on Ethernet with a low-power phone on 2.4 GHz as though they were equivalent.

Do not confuse throughput with responsiveness

Download throughput expresses how quickly bulk data arrives; latency measures how long a response takes, and variation in latency can disrupt calls even when download figures look good. For a home office, a stable 80 Mbps connection can feel better in meetings than a faster link with periodic loss or high delay. If video calls fail, observe whether the issue occurs only while other household members upload large files.

Investigate the local network

Near-router Wi-Fi may still be slower than wired performance because of interference, shared airtime, device capabilities and router processing. At the far end of a hallway, construction materials and distance can be bigger factors. When only one room has trouble, test an alternative router position or a wired access point before paying for a faster ISP tier. When every device slows at the same time, check overall network load and ISP status first.

Keep a small history

Use the Speed Log on SignalNook to store a few controlled observations locally. Note both the location and whether the result came from Ethernet or Wi-Fi, along with any streaming or upload load in the home. Three sensible measurements collected under similar conditions are more actionable than dozens of screenshots without context.

A worked example

Example: a household pays for a 300 Mbps plan. Wired tests average 280 Mbps; Wi-Fi beside the router gives 210 Mbps; a bedroom delivers 25 Mbps. The improvement to investigate is room coverage, not necessarily the broadband plan.

How to interpret three common observations

The same visible symptom can have more than one cause. Use the comparisons below as test priorities, not as an automatic diagnosis of your particular equipment.

Wired matches the plan, distant Wi-Fi is poor

Prioritize placement, the client radio and obstructions. Paying for a faster provider tier would not remove a weak in-home radio path.

Both wired and nearby Wi-Fi slow at one time

Check household bandwidth use, gateway load and provider status. Repeat after the network becomes quiet to confirm the pattern.

A 100 Mbps dongle gives a 90 Mbps result

The adapter or negotiated Ethernet link may be the cap. Check link speed before declaring the ISP or Wi-Fi equipment slow.

Field checklist

  • Run a wired baseline when practical
  • Test near-router Wi-Fi on the same device
  • Repeat from the trouble spot
  • Note upload-heavy activity and time
  • Save readings with the location and connection type
Don't make this mistake

Do not treat the speed displayed on a Wi-Fi link or the advertised router maximum as guaranteed internet throughput.
