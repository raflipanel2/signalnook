Security / GUIDE NO. 20

A 15-minute monthly home Wi-Fi security audit

Keeping a home network safe is more about a repeatable habit than a complex dashboard.

SignalNook EditorialReviewed 11 Nov 2026
THE SHORT ANSWER

Keeping a home network safe is more about a repeatable habit than a complex dashboard. Begin with a repeatable comparison and record one change at a time, rather than making several router changes together.

Check updates at the source

Open your router’s trusted management app or local admin interface using your normal method. Compare firmware with the manufacturer’s support page and enable supported automatic updates where appropriate. For ISP-managed routers, ask the provider how updates are handled. Record when the check happened rather than claiming the router is “up to date forever.”

Inspect connected devices

Read the attached-devices list and compare it with equipment actually in the home. Friendly names can be missing or misleading; an unfamiliar label is not proof of intrusion. Investigate using the manufacturer or device identity, and change credentials if there is a credible concern. Avoid publishing lists of your devices or internal addresses.

Review guest and admin access

Check that guest passwords have not spread farther than intended, and that guests are isolated from the main LAN if your router supports and requires that behavior. Confirm the administrator password is unique. Unless you have a specific supported need, review whether remote management is disabled. Routers differ in terminology, so use vendor documentation.

Look at changes you did not expect

Unexpected DNS, port-forwarding or firewall changes deserve attention, especially if you did not make them. Some are added by legitimate applications or ISP management, so do not delete unfamiliar rules blindly. Compare against your own notes, and seek the router vendor or ISP if you cannot explain an entry.

Track what you reviewed

A small local journal of update checks, guest access and security findings makes future troubleshooting easier. Store no router passwords in browser notes or exported CSV files. If you are responding to a suspected compromise, follow reputable incident-response guidance instead of only rebooting the device.

A worked example

Example: during a monthly check, an old visitor’s device remains on the guest network list. The owner updates the guest password and verifies that the main network remains unchanged.

How to interpret three common observations

The same visible symptom can have more than one cause. Use the comparisons below as test priorities, not as an automatic diagnosis of your particular equipment.

Unknown device appears in client list

Verify vendor and device identity first; names are often misleading. Treat unexplained access as a reason to review credentials.

Remote administration is unexpectedly enabled

Check whether a legitimate support function requires it; otherwise follow vendor guidance to disable external management.

Firmware is out of date and auto-update is off

Plan a supported update and record the review date; do not download firmware from unfamiliar sources.

Field checklist

  • Check firmware and vendor notices
  • Review connected-device list
  • Verify admin and guest access
  • Review unexpected forwarding or DNS changes
  • Record review date without sensitive credentials
Don't make this mistake

Do not share admin passwords, recovery files or complete device inventories with untrusted sites.
